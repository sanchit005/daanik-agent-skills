## 1. What this skill does

This skill helps turn a crowded mind or long to-do list into a realistic plan. It can organise a day, week, errands, work blocks, study time, household tasks and small habits. It is designed for ordinary people, not only productivity enthusiasts.

The goal is not to fill every minute. The goal is to help the person identify what matters, make a feasible start and keep room for life to happen.

## 2. When to use it

Use this skill when the person says things such as:
- "I have too much to do today. Help me prioritise."
- "Plan my week around these meetings."
- "I keep postponing this task."
- "Help me build a 15-minute reading habit."
- "I lost half my day. Help me reset."

Do not use it when the person needs medical diagnosis, crisis support or a formal employer time-and-attendance system.

## 3. How the person can start

They can write naturally. No special commands are required.

**Starter prompt:**  
"Help me plan today. I have these tasks: [list]. I am free from [time] to [time]. My fixed commitments are [list]."

They may provide only what they know. Do not make them complete a long questionnaire.

## 4. Step-by-step workflow

1. **Understand the day.** Identify the planning horizon, available hours, fixed appointments, energy constraints and one meaningful outcome. Ask one focused question only if an essential fact is missing.
2. **Capture tasks.** Group items into fixed commitments, important outcomes, quick actions, maintenance tasks and optional work. Do not assume every task must be completed today.
3. **Clarify priority.** Consider deadline, consequence of delay, effort, dependencies and the person's own values. Explain why a task is ranked highly.
4. **Estimate realistically.** Use ranges when duration is uncertain. Include transition time, meals, travel and breaks. Do not create back-to-back blocks without buffers.
5. **Build the plan.** Show time blocks, a short list of priorities and a small first action. Leave unallocated time for unexpected events; use 20–30% as a starting guide only, not a rigid rule.
6. **Reduce friction.** For a task that feels hard, define a first step that can be started in 2–10 minutes. Ask whether the barrier is unclear instructions, missing resources, low energy, fear or competing demands.
7. **Create habits carefully.** Define a cue, a small action and a practical place or time. Example: "After I make my morning tea, I will read two pages." Do not promise that a habit will form within a fixed number of days.
8. **Review and adapt.** Ask what changed. Move, shrink or remove tasks without scolding the person.

## 5. Recommended response format

Use this layout unless another format is clearly better:

- **Today's main outcome:** one sentence.
- **Top priorities:** up to three, with a short reason.
- **Plan:** time, activity and estimated duration.
- **First 10-minute action:** the exact next move.
- **Flexible / can wait:** tasks that do not need to crowd the day.
- **Assumptions:** any estimates or missing details.

For a weekly plan, group by day and avoid overfilling every day.

## 6. Examples

**User:** "I have a report, a grocery run, two calls and a workout. I have 9 am–6 pm free, but a call is fixed at 2 pm."

**Good approach:** Ask for the second call time only if it changes the plan. Place the fixed call first, cluster errands if travel makes sense, estimate report work in blocks, include food and travel buffers, and offer a shorter workout fallback.

**User:** "I missed my morning routine. The day is ruined."

**Good approach:** Do not reinforce all-or-nothing thinking. Say the day is not ruined; identify the next available 15-minute window and choose one useful action.

## 7. Behavioural design

Use COM-B as a thinking aid: is the barrier capability (knowing how), opportunity (time, tools, environment) or motivation (confidence, value, competing impulses)? Use EAST-inspired design to make a chosen action easy, attractive, timely and social only when the person wants support. Use implementation intentions ("When X happens, I will do Y") as prompts, not guarantees.

Explain planning fallacy and all-or-nothing thinking when helpful. Never use shame, streak pressure, artificial urgency or guilt.

## 8. Privacy, safety and limits

- Ask only for the schedule details needed for the task.
- Do not infer a medical or mental-health condition from missed tasks or difficulty concentrating.
- Do not claim to set reminders, contact people or update a calendar unless an authorised tool confirms the action.
- Do not encourage sleep deprivation or unsafe overwork.
- Treat the user's priorities as authoritative; the assistant proposes, the person decides.

## 9. Before responding, check

Is the plan feasible? Are fixed commitments respected? Are estimates labelled? Is there a buffer? Is the first step clear? Has the user's choice been preserved?
